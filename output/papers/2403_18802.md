## Benchmark Modalities

**Modality Types:** text

**Description:** Text-only prompts that solicit long-form text responses from the evaluated models, verified against web-search text snippets.

## Analytical Tasks

**Task Types:** factual recall, open ended qa, generation, fact verification

**Detailed Description:** The benchmark tests factual recall through open-ended question answering and long-form text generation. The evaluation process decomposes the generated responses into individual facts and verifies them using an external search-augmented pipeline (SAFE) for fact verification.

## Summary

This paper presents LongFact, a multi-topic dataset of 2,280 prompts across 38 topics designed to benchmark the long-form factuality of large language models. To enable scalable evaluation, the authors introduce SAFE (Search-Augmented Factuality Evaluator), an agentic framework that utilizes an LLM to split a response into atomic facts and verify them recursively via Google Search queries. The authors also propose the F1@K metric, which adapts standard F1 to balance factual precision with recall mapped against a user-preferred response length.

## Key Findings

The LLM agent-based evaluator SAFE achieves high agreement (72%) with human crowdsourced annotators and wins 76% of disagreements in expert reviews, while being over 20 times cheaper ($0.19 vs. $4.00 per response). Multi-model benchmarking on LongFact shows that larger models generally exhibit superior long-form factuality, with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) heavily improving factual recall.

**Facets Explanation:** LongFact uses open-ended prompts without predefined static answers. Instead, the SAFE framework dynamically determines the factual ground truth by using an LLM agent to extract atomic facts and verify them via Google Search (model_generated). Generating responses requires external knowledge not supplied in the query (gold_beyond_stimulus), and evaluation relies on LLM judgments rather than a fixed key (graded_human_judgment). Disagreements between human annotators and the automated rater were treated as errors and resolved through expert adjudication to determine a single correct label (noise).
